The Falafel’s walk on the square spiral never ends

Martin Norbäck Olivers

Draft, 28 November 2026

Abstract. The Falafel is the chess piece that moves one or two squares diagonally. Put it on square 1 of the square spiral and let it always move to the lowest-numbered square that it has not yet visited. The numbers of the squares it visits form sequence A399160 of the OEIS. We prove that the Falafel is never trapped and that it visits every square it can reach, so the sequence is a permutation of the odd positive integers. After its first 80 squares the walk runs in laps around the origin, and we give every lap in closed form. The proof is a case analysis in linear integer arithmetic. It has been formalised in Lean 4 and is checked by the Lean kernel.

1. Introduction

Number the squares of an infinite chessboard along the square spiral: square 1 is \((0,0)\), square 2 is \((1,0)\), square 3 is \((1,1)\), and the numbering goes on counter-clockwise (Figure 1). Put a chess piece on square 1 and let it move by one rule: it always moves to the lowest-numbered square that it has not visited before. For the knight this walk ends. At step 2016 the knight stands on square 2084, and every square one knight move away has been visited [2].

Here the piece is the Falafel. It is the compound of the Fers, which moves one square diagonally, and the Alfil, which leaps two squares diagonally. The numbers of the squares it visits are sequence A399160 of the OEIS [1], contributed by J. Ahlström:

\[ 1,\ 3,\ 7,\ 19,\ 5,\ 9,\ 11,\ 15,\ 33,\ 13,\ 29,\ 53,\ \dots \]

On 23 November 2026 the entry did not say whether the sequence is finite. We prove that it is infinite, and we determine it completely.

Write \(\sigma(x,y)\) for the number of the square \((x,y)\); the formula is (1) in Section 2. By construction, every positive integer is the number of exactly one square. Two squares \((x,y)\) and \((x',y')\) are one move apart if \(|x'-x| = |y'-y|\) and this common value is 1 or 2.

Definition 1. A Falafel walk is an infinite sequence \(a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots\) of squares such that

  1. (W1) \(a_0 = (0,0)\);
  2. (W2) \(a_m \ne a_n\) whenever \(m \ne n\);
  3. (W3) \(a_{n+1}\) is one move from \(a_n\), for every \(n\);
  4. (W4) for every \(n\), every square \(q\) that is one move from \(a_n\) and has \(\sigma(q) < \sigma(a_{n+1})\) is one of \(a_0, \dots, a_n\).

Conditions (W2) to (W4) say that \(a_{n+1}\) is the lowest-numbered unvisited square one move from \(a_n\). A trapped piece produces only a finite sequence, so a Falafel walk exists exactly if the Falafel is never trapped. The \(n\)-th term of A399160 is \(\sigma(a_{n-1})\).

Remark 1(only one walk). Different squares have different numbers, so the rule allows at most one move: \(a_0, \dots, a_n\) determine \(a_{n+1}\). So there is at most one Falafel walk.

Theorem 1.

  1. (a) There is a Falafel walk.
  2. (b) It visits every square \((x,y)\) with \(x+y\) even, and no other square.

The squares with \(x+y\) even are the squares with odd numbers. So A399160 is a permutation of the odd positive integers (Corollary 8 in Section 6).

Theorem 2 in Section 3 describes the walk completely. Its first 80 squares are irregular. After them the walk runs in laps \(R = 4, 5, 6, \dots\) around the origin, and one closed formula gives the squares of every lap. Corollary 9 in Section 6 turns this into a formula for the \(n\)-th term of the sequence.

The proof goes as follows. Every square gets a stamp: the number of its lap, and its index within the lap. Four lemmas about stamps carry the proof (Section 4). Each is a statement of linear integer arithmetic and is proved by a case analysis, which we do not print. The whole proof has been formalised in Lean 4 [3], and the Lean kernel has checked it. Sections 5 and 6 derive the theorems and Corollaries 8 and 9 from the lemmas by hand. Section 7 says what was formalised, and which few lines a reader has to trust.

2. The spiral in diagonal coordinates

Let \(r = \max(|x|,|y|)\) be the ring of the square \((x,y)\). The spiral number is

\[ \sigma(x,y) \;=\; (2r-1)^2 + \begin{cases} y + r & \text{if } x = r \text{ and } y > -r, \\ 2r + (r - x) & \text{if } y = r, \\ 4r + (r - y) & \text{if } x = -r, \\ 6r + (x + r) & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} \]
(1)

where the first case that applies is taken. Ring 0 is the square \((0,0)\) with number 1. Ring \(r \ge 1\) holds the numbers from \((2r-1)^2 + 1\) to \((2r+1)^2\).

A move changes \(x\) by \(\pm 1\) or \(\pm 2\) and \(y\) by the same amount up to sign. So a move does not change the parity of \(x+y\), and a Falafel walk, which starts at \((0,0)\), stays on the squares with \(x+y\) even.

Definition 2(diagonal coordinates). The diagonal coordinates of a square \((x,y)\) with \(x+y\) even are the integers

\[ u = \frac{x+y}{2}, \qquad v = \frac{x-y}{2}, \qquad\text{so that}\qquad (x,y) = (u+v,\ u-v). \]

Every pair \((u,v)\) of integers belongs to exactly one such square. A step is one of the pairs \((\pm 1, 0)\), \((0, \pm 1)\), \((\pm 2, 0)\), \((0, \pm 2)\).

From now on, except in Section 6, a square is given by its diagonal coordinates: it is a pair \(s = (u,v)\) of integers, and \(\sigma(s)\) means \(\sigma(u+v,\,u-v)\). Two squares are one move apart exactly if their difference is a step. So in diagonal coordinates the Falafel is a rook that moves one or two squares.

Definition 3(ring and position). For a square \((u,v)\) put \(\rho(u,v) = @@RHO@@\) and

\[ \pi(u,v) \;=\; @@PI_CASES@@ \]

Lemma 1. Let \(s\) and \(s'\) be squares.

  1. (i) \(\sigma(s) = (2\rho(s)-1)^2 + \pi(s)\).
  2. (ii) \(0 \le \pi(s) \le 8\rho(s)\), and \(\pi(s) \ge 1\) if \(\rho(s) \ge 1\).
  3. (iii) If \(\rho(s) < \rho(s')\), or \(\rho(s) = \rho(s')\) and \(\pi(s) \le \pi(s')\), then \(\sigma(s) \le \sigma(s')\).

Proof. Since \(\max(|u+v|,|u-v|) = |u|+|v|\), the number \(\rho(s)\) is the ring of \(s\). The cases of (1) are the four sides of the ring. On each side, putting \(x = u+v\) and \(y = u-v\) into (1) gives the stated value of \(\pi\). This is (i), and (ii) can be read off from the cases of \(\pi\). For (iii), let \(\rho(s) < \rho(s')\). Then by (i) and (ii)

\[ \sigma(s) \;\le\; (2\rho(s)-1)^2 + 8\rho(s) \;=\; (2\rho(s)+1)^2 \;\le\; (2\rho(s')-1)^2 \;<\; \sigma(s'). \]

If the rings are equal, (iii) is (i).□

3. The walk

Definition 4(laps). For an integer \(R \ge 4\) put

\[ \mathrm{lo}(R) = @@LO_CASES@@ \qquad \mathrm{hi}(R) = @@HI_CASES@@ \]

Lap \(R\) is the sequence of squares \(P_R(j)\) for \(\mathrm{lo}(R) \le j < \mathrm{hi}(R)\), where \(P_R(j)\) is given by Table 1.

A lap consists of nine pieces, A to I; Figure 2 shows lap 6. Piece A runs along the row \(v = R\) (for odd \(R\) it starts one square below that row). Pieces B, D, F and H are staircases along the four sides of a diamond, each one ring further out than the one before, and C, E and G are the corners between them. Piece I comes back in along a slanted strip and ends one step from the start of lap \(R+1\). The indices are counted from the start of piece B, so the indices of piece A are negative.

Let \(T_0, T_1, \dots, T_{79}\) be the 80 squares of Table 4 in Appendix A.

Theorem 2. Let \(W_0, W_1, W_2, \dots\) be the sequence that consists of \(T_0, \dots, T_{79}\), then lap 4, then lap 5, and so on.

  1. (a) Every square \((u,v)\) of \(\mathbb{Z}^2\) occurs exactly once in this sequence.
  2. (b) Write \(W_n = (u_n, v_n)\). Then \(a_n = (u_n + v_n,\ u_n - v_n)\) is the Falafel walk.

The number of squares of lap \(R\) is

\[ \mathrm{hi}(R) - \mathrm{lo}(R) = @@LEN_CASES@@ \]

So lap 4 starts with \(W_{80}\) and lap 5 with \(W_{133}\). Corollary 9 gives the start of every lap.

A program checked the following, and the description of the pieces above, for \(4 \le R \le @@RMAX@@\); it is not part of the formal proof. Lap \(R\) lies in the rings \(R \le \rho \le 2R + 2\) and has squares in both of these rings. So when lap \(R\) is complete, every ring up to \(R\) is fully visited, ring \(R + 1\) is not, and no square beyond ring \(2R + 2\) has been visited. The outermost visited ring is about twice the first incomplete one.

4. Stamps

Table 1 gives the square at a given index of a lap. The next definition goes the other way: it gives the lap and the index of a given square (Lemma 3).

Definition 5(lap number and index). The lap number \(\lambda(u,v)\) of a square is given by Table 2. For a square with \(\lambda(u,v) \ge 4\), its index \(\kappa(u,v)\) is given by Table 5 in Appendix A.

The ten regions of Table 2 and the centre are disjoint and cover the plane. Regions 1 to 8 hold the pieces A to H, and region 10 holds piece I; region 9 holds the first two squares of an odd lap. A value below 4 is not the number of a lap: by Lemma 2 it marks one of the first 80 squares. Figure 3 shows the lap numbers near the origin.

Lemma 2(the first 80 squares). The squares \(T_0, \dots, T_{79}\) are distinct. A square \(s\) has \(\lambda(s) \le 3\) exactly if it is one of them.

Lemma 3(indices). Let \(s\) be a square with \(\lambda(s) = R \ge 4\). Then \(\mathrm{lo}(R) \le \kappa(s) < \mathrm{hi}(R)\) and \(P_R(\kappa(s)) = s\).

Example. The square \(s = (-5,-2)\) lies in region 4. Tables 2 and 5 give

\[ \lambda(s) = \lfloor (1+5+2)/2 \rfloor = 4, \qquad \kappa(s) = 4 \cdot 4 - 2 - 5 + 2 = 11. \]

In Table 1 with \(R = 4\), the index \(11 = 2R + 2 + 1\) belongs to piece D with \(t = 1\), and

\[ P_4(11) = (2 - 8 + \lfloor 2/2 \rfloor,\ -2 - \lfloor 1/2 \rfloor) = (-5,-2). \]

Definition 6(stamps). The stamp of a square \(s\) is the pair

\[ \tau(s) = \begin{cases} (3,\ i) & \text{if } s = T_i, \\ (\lambda(s),\ \kappa(s)) & \text{if } \lambda(s) \ge 4. \end{cases} \]

Stamps are ordered lexicographically. Put \(\mathrm{lo}(3) = 0\) and \(\mathrm{hi}(3) = 80\), and let \(S\) be the set of pairs \((R,j)\) of integers with \(R \ge 3\) and \(\mathrm{lo}(R) \le j < \mathrm{hi}(R)\). The successor of \((R,j)\) in \(S\) is \((R, j+1)\) if \(j + 1 < \mathrm{hi}(R)\), and \((R+1, \mathrm{lo}(R+1))\) otherwise.

By Lemma 2 every square has a stamp.

Corollary 4. Every stamp lies in \(S\), and different squares have different stamps.

Proof. The first claim is Lemma 2 for stamps \((3,i)\) and Lemma 3 for the others. Let \(\tau(s) = \tau(s')\). If this stamp is \((3,i)\), then \(s = T_i = s'\). If it is \((R,j)\) with \(R \ge 4\), then \(s = P_R(j) = s'\) by Lemma 3.□

Definition 7(next square). The next square \(s^+\) of a square \(s\) is

\[ s^+ = \begin{cases} T_{i+1} & \text{if } s = T_i \text{ with } i < 79, \\ P_4(\mathrm{lo}(4)) & \text{if } s = T_{79}, \\ P_R(\kappa(s)+1) & \text{if } \lambda(s) = R \ge 4 \text{ and } \kappa(s) + 1 < \mathrm{hi}(R), \\ P_{R+1}(\mathrm{lo}(R+1)) & \text{if } \lambda(s) = R \ge 4 \text{ and } \kappa(s) + 1 = \mathrm{hi}(R). \end{cases} \]

By Lemmas 2 and 3 exactly one case applies.

Lemma 5(the next square). For every square \(s\), the difference \(s^+ - s\) is a step, and \(\tau(s^+)\) is the successor of \(\tau(s)\) in \(S\).

Lemma 6(the greedy rule). Let \(s\) be a square and \(e\) a step. If \(\tau(s+e) > \tau(s)\), then \(\sigma(s^+) \le \sigma(s+e)\).

In words: a square one step from \(s\) with a later stamp never has a lower number than the next square \(s^+\).

On the proofs of Lemmas 2, 3, 5 and 6. We do not print them. A case check on Table 2 shows that all squares with \(\lambda \le 3\) lie in the box \(-4 \le u \le 6\), \(-5 \le v \le 4\). For them the lemmas are checked by evaluation. For a square with \(\lambda(s) \ge 4\) the proof splits into cases: by the region of \(s\), by the rows of Tables 2 and 5, by the remainders these rows use, and in Lemmas 5 and 6 also by the region of the second square. In every case each quantity is an explicit expression in \(u\) and \(v\) that is linear apart from division by 2, 3 or 6 with remainder. So every case is a statement of linear arithmetic over the integers, and such statements can be decided mechanically. Lemma 6 compares spiral numbers, which are quadratic. By Lemma 1 (iii) it is enough to compare rings and positions, and these are linear. In Lean every case is closed by omega, a decision procedure for linear integer arithmetic, and the statements about the first 80 squares by decide, which evaluates them inside the kernel.

5. Proof of Theorems 1 and 2

Let \(V_0 = (0,0) = T_0\) and \(V_{n+1} = V_n^+\).

Step 1. The stamps \(\tau(V_0), \tau(V_1), \dots\) are the elements of \(S\) in increasing order, each once. For every \(R \ge 3\) the number \(\mathrm{hi}(R) - \mathrm{lo}(R)\) is positive. So \((3,0)\) is the least element of \(S\), and every element of \(S\) is reached from \((3,0)\) by taking the successor finitely often. Now \(\tau(V_0) = (3,0)\), and \(\tau(V_{n+1})\) is the successor of \(\tau(V_n)\) by Lemma 5.

Step 2. \(V_n = W_n\) for all \(n\), and Theorem 2 (a) holds. Let \(s\) be a square. By Corollary 4 and Step 1 there is exactly one \(n\) with \(\tau(V_n) = \tau(s)\), and then \(V_n = s\) because different squares have different stamps. So every square occurs exactly once among the \(V_n\). The square with stamp \((3,i)\) is \(T_i\). The square with stamp \((R,j)\), \(R \ge 4\), is \(P_R(j)\) by Lemma 3. Since the stamps come in increasing order, the sequence \(V\) is \(T_0, \dots, T_{79}\), then lap 4, then lap 5, and so on. This is the sequence \(W\).

Step 3. \(W\) satisfies (W1) to (W4), with “differs by a step” for “one move apart”. (W1) holds by definition, (W2) by Step 2 and (W3) by Lemma 5. For (W4) let \(e\) be a step and \(q = W_n + e\) with \(\sigma(q) < \sigma(W_{n+1})\). Since \(W_{n+1} = W_n^+\), Lemma 6 shows that \(\tau(q) > \tau(W_n)\) is false. So \(\tau(q) \le \tau(W_n)\). By Step 2, \(q = W_m\) for some \(m\), and by Step 1, \(m \le n\).

By Section 2 the sequence \(a_n = (u_n + v_n,\ u_n - v_n)\) is therefore a Falafel walk. This proves Theorem 1 (a). By Remark 1 it is the only Falafel walk, which is Theorem 2 (b). By Step 2 it visits every square with \(x+y\) even, and by Section 2 it visits no other square. This is Theorem 1 (b).□

6. The sequence

In Lemma 7 and Corollary 8 a square is again a pair \((x,y)\), with any parity of \(x+y\).

Lemma 7. For every square \((x,y)\), \(\sigma(x,y) \equiv x + y + 1 \pmod 2\).

Proof. Each square of the spiral shares a side with the next one. So from one square to the next the number goes up by 1 and \(x + y\) changes by 1, and \(\sigma(x,y) - x - y\) keeps its parity. At the square \((0,0)\) it is 1.□

Corollary 8. Let \(a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots\) be the Falafel walk. Every odd positive integer is \(\sigma(a_n)\) for exactly one \(n\), and every \(\sigma(a_n)\) is odd. So A399160 is a permutation of the odd positive integers.

Proof. Let \(k\) be an odd positive integer. It is the number of a square \((x,y)\), and \(x + y\) is even by Lemma 7. By Theorem 1 (b) there is \(n\) with \(a_n = (x,y)\), so \(\sigma(a_n) = k\). If also \(\sigma(a_m) = k\), then \(a_m = a_n\), because different squares have different numbers, and \(m = n\) by (W2). Finally, every \(a_n\) has \(x + y\) even, so every \(\sigma(a_n)\) is odd by Lemma 7.□

Corollary 9(the terms). For an integer \(R \ge 4\) put

\[ \mathrm{start}(R) = @@START_CASES@@ \]

Lap \(R\) starts with \(W_{\mathrm{start}(R)}\). So \(W_n = T_n\) for \(n < 80\). For \(n \ge 80\) let \(R\) be the largest integer with \(\mathrm{start}(R) \le n\). Then \(W_n\) lies in lap \(R\), and

\[ W_n = P_R\bigl(\mathrm{lo}(R) + n - \mathrm{start}(R)\bigr). \]

The \((n+1)\)-th term of A399160 is \((2\rho(W_n) - 1)^2 + \pi(W_n)\). Table 6 in Appendix A gives this number as a function of \(R\) and the index \(j = \mathrm{lo}(R) + n - \mathrm{start}(R)\).

Proof. By Theorem 2 the squares of the walk are \(W_0, W_1, W_2, \dots\), and Lemma 1 (i) gives the number of \(W_n\). Lap 4 starts with \(W_{80}\), and \(\mathrm{start}(4) = 80\). For both parities of \(R\), the difference \(\mathrm{start}(R+1) - \mathrm{start}(R)\) is the number \(\mathrm{hi}(R) - \mathrm{lo}(R)\) of squares of lap \(R\) (Section 3). So lap \(R\) starts with \(W_{\mathrm{start}(R)}\) and ends just before \(W_{\mathrm{start}(R+1)}\). The numbers \(\mathrm{start}(R)\) increase with \(R\). So \(W_n\) with \(n \ge 80\) lies in lap \(R\) exactly if \(R\) is the largest integer with \(\mathrm{start}(R) \le n\), and then it is the square of lap \(R\) at index \(\mathrm{lo}(R) + n - \mathrm{start}(R)\). On every row of Table 1 the ring \(\rho\) and the position \(\pi\) are linear in \(R\) and \(j\), and Table 6 is \((2\rho - 1)^2 + \pi\) for that row.□

7. The formal proof

The proof has been formalised in Lean 4, version 4.36.0, with no library beyond Lean itself. The Lean kernel accepts it. It has no sorry, which would mark an unproved step, and no native_decide, which would take the result of compiled code on trust. It depends only on the three standard axioms propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound. The Lean files, with the program that printed the tables of this paper, are in the repository [4].

What a reader has to trust. Appendix B reproduces the statement as Lean has it. It defines \(\sigma\) by formula (1), a move, and a Falafel walk as in Definition 1, and it states four claims: Remark 1, Theorem 1 (a), Theorem 1 (b) and Corollary 8. It uses a few general notions (absolute value, even, odd, “exactly one”), which a short file of the proof defines in the usual way, one line each; a reader trusts that file too. The laps, the lap number, the index and the stamps are only tools of the proof. A mistake in them could make the proof fail. It could not make a false theorem pass. Corollary 9 is proved in Lean as a formula for the terms: for every \(n \ge 80\) the number of \(a_n\) is the entry of Table 6 (term_formula). Its statement is in a second file, which defines \(\mathrm{start}\), \(\mathrm{lo}\) and the numbers of Table 6 and mentions no square and no lap. Appendix B shows it without the rows of the table. A reader who wants that claim has to trust that file as well.

What corresponds to what. Table 3 lists the Lean theorem behind each statement of this paper. Lean also proves that the first 70 squares of its walk have the 70 numbers listed in the OEIS entry (first_terms). This checks that Definition 1 is the rule of the OEIS entry.

Where Lean and this paper differ. Lean defines the next square by a table of moves, one move for every square, arranged by region like Table 5. It proves that this move is a step and leads to the square with the next stamp. With Lemma 3 this is Lemma 5. Lean also proves that a lap is exactly a level set of the lap number: for \(R \ge 4\), \(\lambda(s) = R\) exactly if \(s = P_R(j)\) for some \(j\) with \(\mathrm{lo}(R) \le j < \mathrm{hi}(R)\) (lapnum_eq_iff). Lean has the numbering only as formula (1). So it also proves, as far as the proof needs it, what this paper takes from the construction of the spiral: different squares with \(x+y\) even have different numbers, every number is positive, and every positive integer is the number of a square (the first row of Table 3). It proves Lemma 7 from the four cases of (1).

What is not formalised. The tables of this paper are a transcription of the Lean definitions. A program printed them from expressions that it first compared with the Lean definitions: Tables 2 and 5, \(\rho\) and \(\pi\) for all squares with \(|u|, |v| \le @@BOX@@\), and Table 1 with \(\mathrm{lo}\) and \(\mathrm{hi}\) for the laps \(4 \le R \le @@RMAX@@\). The same program compared the sequence \(W\) of Theorem 2 with a direct simulation of the rule for the first @@SIM@@ steps, and it tested Lemmas 2, 3, 5 and 6 in the wording of this paper for all squares with \(|u|, |v| \le @@LBOX@@\). It also checked that formula (1) gives the numbers of the first @@NSPIRAL@@ squares along the spiral. The description of a lap and of the visited rings in Section 3 is not formalised either. The program compared Table 6 with the Lean table of the terms and with the numbers of the squares of Table 1 for \(4 \le R \le @@RMAX@@\), and with the simulation. It compared the formula for \(\mathrm{start}(R)\) with the Lean definition, which is one expression for both parities of \(R\), and with the lengths of the laps for \(4 \le R \le @@SMAX@@\).
